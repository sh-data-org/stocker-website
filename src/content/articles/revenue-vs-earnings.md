## Two different questions

Revenue describes the money a company recognizes from selling goods or services. Earnings usually refers to profit, but the word needs a qualifier: operating earnings, net earnings and adjusted earnings are not the same measure.

When comparing the two, ask: “Which earnings figure, for which period, under which accounting basis?” A headline that says earnings grew may be discussing per-share profit rather than total net income.

## Follow a fictional income statement

Consider a small fictional business over one year:

| Line                                |     Amount |
| ----------------------------------- | ---------: |
| Revenue                             | $1,000,000 |
| Cost of providing its products      |   $400,000 |
| Other operating expenses            |   $350,000 |
| Operating profit                    |   $250,000 |
| Net interest and income tax expense |    $70,000 |
| Net earnings                        |   $180,000 |

The company brought in $1 million of recognized sales, but its net earnings were $180,000. This simplified example is not a template for every industry; actual statements contain additional lines and accounting judgments.

![Illustration of business activity flowing past operating costs to a smaller remaining amount](../../assets/article-business.png)

_Revenue is the starting point. Earnings reflect the costs and other items recognized along the way._

## Sales can rise while profit falls

Suppose revenue grows from $1 million to $1.2 million next year. That sounds encouraging. But if the combined costs rise from $820,000 to $1.08 million, net earnings fall from $180,000 to $120,000.

The next question is why. The company might be investing in a new product, facing higher input costs, discounting to retain customers, or experiencing a combination of factors. The growth percentage alone cannot choose among those explanations.

A useful research note states both the change and the explanation you need to verify. “Revenue rose; determine whether the lower margin comes from temporary investment or a lasting cost change” is more useful than “growth is good.”

## Keep comparisons consistent

Compare quarterly figures with the corresponding quarter when seasonality matters. Compare annual figures with annual figures. Check whether acquisitions, currency movements or accounting changes affect the interpretation.

Do not treat adjusted profit and reported profit as equivalent. Read the reconciliation and understand what was excluded. Also remember that recognized revenue is not always cash received in the same period, and earnings are not the same as cash flow.
