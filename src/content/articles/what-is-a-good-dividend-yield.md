A good dividend yield starts with a payment the company can sustain. Compare it with businesses in the same industry, then ask what drove the yield and how readily the company can fund the dividend. A yield can climb from **4% to 8%** with the same cash payment per share.

## How a 4% yield becomes 8%

Dividend yield divides annual dividends per share by the current share price. Consider a company that paid a total of $2 per share over the past year:

| Scenario                                               | Annual dividend per share | Share price | Dividend yield |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------: | ----------: | -------------: |
| Before the share price falls                           |                     $2.00 |      $50.00 |             4% |
| After the share price halves                           |                     $2.00 |      $25.00 |             8% |
| If the company later cuts its annualized payment to $1 |                     $1.00 |      $25.00 |             4% |

In the second row, the screen shows a higher yield while the company still paid $2. The third row shows what happens to the indicated yield if the company subsequently cuts its dividend and the stock price stays at $25. A quote based on the last twelve months of payments can keep showing the older, higher figure for a while. Try both figures in the [dividend yield calculator](/tools/dividend-yield-calculator/) to see the difference.

## Check the payout behind the yield

1. **Look up the last dividend declaration.** Confirm the regular payment, how often it is paid, and whether management announced a cut or suspension. Check whether the past year included a one-time special dividend.
2. **Compare dividends with earnings.** If a company paid $2 per share and earned $3 per share over the same period, it paid out about **67%** of those earnings. If earnings were $1.50, the same $2 dividend would represent about **133%**. That calls for a closer look at what drove the low earnings and how long the cash can cover the gap.
3. **Check the cash flow and balance sheet.** See how much cash remains after capital spending and compare it with the cash dividends paid over the same period. Check upcoming debt payments and look across several years for a pattern. A single period can reflect a major investment or a temporary change in working capital.

Some industries require a different starting point. A property REIT, for example, may report funds from operations alongside earnings. Use comparable figures within the industry and read how each company defines the measure. ## Compare yields using the same inputs

Use regular dividends on the same basis for both stocks. A **trailing yield** uses payments made over the last twelve months. An **indicated yield** annualizes the latest regular payment if it is expected to recur. Record the share-price date and dividend basis so you can compare the figures again after earnings.

If one stock has a much higher yield than similar companies, look for the reason in its latest results. Then save the dividend declaration, payout ratio and next reporting date in your [stock research worksheet](/tools/research-template/).
