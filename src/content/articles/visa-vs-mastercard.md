## Understand what you are comparing

Visa and Mastercard are payment-network businesses. Their brands appear during everyday purchases, but the company behind the network should not be confused with every bank, merchant or payment provider participating in a transaction.

A useful stock comparison begins by mapping those roles. Then it examines the companies' actual disclosures, reporting periods and valuation assumptions. This page does not identify a preferred investment or publish a live financial ranking.

## Map the economic questions

| Area                   | What to investigate for each company                                        |
| ---------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Network activity       | Which transaction and payment-volume measures does it report?               |
| Geographic exposure    | How much does the business depend on domestic versus cross-border activity? |
| Additional services    | What activities extend beyond the core payment network?                     |
| Customer relationships | How do contracts, incentives and partners affect the economics?             |
| Risk                   | What regulatory, competitive and operational issues appear in the filings?  |

Visa's corporate description includes payment connectivity and services such as fraud management, data products and consulting. Mastercard's investor materials provide the counterpart evidence for researching its own activities. Similar product language should lead to a closer comparison, not an assumption of identical economics.

## Read the definitions beside the numbers

A percentage change is only useful when you understand what changed. Volume, transaction count, revenue and earnings measure different things. Currency effects, geographic mix and business mix can influence the relationship between them.

Use the same period and distinguish reported figures from adjusted or currency-neutral measures. If a company explains a change through several drivers, record those drivers rather than reducing the story to one growth rate.

## Avoid the familiar-brand shortcut

A product you recognize can be a good starting point for curiosity. It cannot establish whether the company's shares are reasonably priced or appropriate for a particular portfolio.

Write a short explanation of how the business gets paid, then list the assumptions that would have to hold for its economics to improve. Repeat the exercise for the other company. The differences between those notes tell you what to investigate next.

## Build a dated comparison

Read the original annual reports and recent results. Put your observations in a [research worksheet](/tools/research-template/) with source links and dates. A comparison without dates can silently mix information from different market conditions.
