## Follow the payment network

Visa describes a network connecting consumers, merchants, financial institutions and other partners. Its business is easier to understand when you separate that network role from the businesses that issue a card, accept a payment or provide a bank account.

The logo you see at checkout identifies part of a larger payment system. It does not tell you how every fee in the transaction is divided among participants.

## Who pays Visa, and for what?

Visa earns fees from clients, including financial institutions, for access to its payment services, transaction processing and related services. It does not issue the cards or earn the cardholder's borrowing interest.

Its fiscal 2026 annual report separates revenue into four categories:

- **Service revenue:** fees supporting use of Visa payment services, primarily linked to payment volume, plus some issuing services.
- **Data processing:** authorization, clearing and settlement, network support, and services such as risk and security products.
- **International transactions:** cross-border processing and currency conversion activities.
- **Other revenue:** advisory services, licensing and certain other services.

Client incentives generally reduce the revenue Visa reports. Value-added services appear within several revenue categories; adding them again would double-count activity. [Visa's revenue-recognition notes](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1403161/000140316126000134/v-20260930.htm) explain the accounting.

## Avoid the checkout-fee shortcut

A merchant's total card-acceptance cost is not all Visa revenue. Interchange generally flows from the merchant's acquiring institution to the card issuer. Visa occupies a different role in the chain.

For research, draw the participants separately: shopper, merchant, issuer, acquirer and network. That makes it easier to see which company's economics you are analyzing.

## Questions to bring to the reports

| Question                                 | Why it helps                                                              |
| ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| What payment activity is being measured? | Payment value and transaction count describe different things             |
| What is the geographic mix?              | Different payment flows can have different economics                      |
| What services are growing?               | The business can change beyond the core network activity                  |
| What incentives and expenses matter?     | Gross activity does not translate mechanically into net revenue or profit |
| What risks does management disclose?     | A familiar consumer brand does not eliminate business uncertainty         |

The table is a research agenda. It is not a statement that a particular metric is improving or a substitute for reading the current numbers.

## Connect activity to financial results

When you see payment-volume growth, ask how it relates to recognized revenue and earnings. The answer can depend on mix, pricing, contractual terms and other factors discussed in the reports.

Keep periods aligned and distinguish management's explanation from your own interpretation. Our [revenue vs earnings guide](/learn/revenue-vs-earnings/) explains why growing business activity and growing net profit are not interchangeable statements.

## Put the business in context

A comparison with another network can help you identify which questions are company-specific and which apply more broadly. Read [Visa vs Mastercard](/compare/visa-vs-mastercard/) as a framework for that investigation, not a stock recommendation.

Use a [research worksheet](/tools/research-template/) to record sources and unanswered questions. If a result or announcement changes your view, note exactly which assumption changed. A clearer business model is valuable even when the next action is simply to keep learning.
